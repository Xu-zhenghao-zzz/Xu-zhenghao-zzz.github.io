Research Portfolio

My research interests lie at the intersection of learning algorithms, agentic AI systems, and experimental systems.

My current work can be broadly organized into three directions:

  1. Reinforcement Learning & Graph-Structured Decision Making
  2. Multimodal Models & AI Evaluation
  3. LLM Agents & Automated AI Systems

Some of my earlier research uses networking and wireless systems as experimental environments. I view these projects primarily as studies of reinforcement learning, structured decision making, experimental methodology, and system behavior rather than communication systems as a long-term research direction.

Reinforcement Learning & Graph Decision Making

GRACE: Graph-aware Joint Decision Making with GNN and PPO

Manuscript under revision

This work formulates a topology-structured joint decision problem as sequential decision making over graphs. Graph representations encode structural information, while PPO is used to learn topology-aware policies.

My contributions include: designing cross-topology generalization experiments; constructing unseen-topology evaluation settings; organizing parameter sensitivity studies; and analyzing robustness and degradation under changing environments.

The framework and core algorithm were not proposed or led by me, so my contribution is intentionally scoped to experimental design and generalization evaluation.

Joint Decision Making in Dynamic UAV Environments

Manuscript under revision

This is a Python-based research project for which I have taken a leading implementation role. The project studies multi-variable joint decision making in dynamic environments, currently using computation placement and routing in UAV networks as the experimental setting.

My work includes: problem formulation, simulation environment design, core Python implementation, experimental protocol design, parameter sweeps, and result analysis. The project has provided end-to-end experience in turning a research problem into a reproducible experimental study.

Multimodal AI & Evaluation

RobustVidBench

Manuscript

RobustVidBench is a benchmark for studying the robustness of multimodal video understanding models under realistic spatial and temporal degradations. Evaluation factors include bitrate, resolution, frame rate, frame loss, and audio-video synchronization.

Dataset construction uses a semi-automated annotation pipeline combining model cross-checking with human verification.

My contributions include: benchmark construction support, multimodal data processing, annotation workflow implementation, pipeline execution, testing code, and robustness evaluation.

This project has given me practical experience in MLLM evaluation, benchmark construction, and large-scale multimodal data processing.

Automated Video Understanding Dataset Construction

Manuscript in preparation

We developed a three-stage automated Video-to-QA pipeline: (1) build structured knowledge and retrieval-augmented context; (2) extract visual frames and audio information; (3) use LLM agents to generate Video-QA candidates followed by human verification.

The current system has processed approximately 600+ videos, 10,000+ QA pairs, and 10+ controlled experimental comparisons.

My work primarily covers data processing, pipeline execution, model inference, testing, and experimental analysis.

LLM Agents & Experimental AI Systems

A Minimal Diversity Instruction Repairs LLM Experimental Design

Manuscript

This work asks a broader question: can an LLM directly select sufficiently informative experimental interventions when interacting with a complex real-world system? We use a real system stack as a controlled environment and study the behavior of LLMs when they are asked to design experiments directly.

Our experiments suggest that a baseline-plus-single-factor-flip strategy can systematically miss important variable combinations. A short diversity-oriented instruction changes the geometry of the selected interventions and improves average recovery for some tested targets.

The broader AI questions behind the study include LLM experimental design, agent behavior, exploration bias, intervention selection, and AI-assisted scientific workflows.

We intentionally keep the claims narrow and do not present the empirical result as a general optimal experimental-design theorem.

Systems Experiments & Measurement

The following projects come from my earlier work on experimental systems. Although they use wireless or networking environments, I view them primarily as experience in experimental design, mechanism analysis, causal intervention, and systems evaluation, rather than as my main long-term research direction.

Compression Placement Is Not Free

Manuscript

This study examines how relocating computation or compression can affect other tasks through shared system resources. The experimental methodology includes paired interventions, controlled padding, computation-delay controls, and boundary scans, designed to distinguish competing causal mechanisms.

When the Network Chooses the Data

Manuscript

This work studies whether system execution mechanisms can alter which data actually participates in a machine-learning process and, consequently, affect model outcomes. The study focuses on causal intervention, participation bias, counterfactual comparisons, and negative results for tested correction approaches.

Limits of Recipient-Blind Pricing

Manuscript

This work studies informational limitations of fixed local decision rules under partial observability. The main conceptual questions involve partial observability, local decision rules, globally optimal actions, and information limitations.

Which Channel Sees the Victims?

Manuscript

This work compares different environment representations for predicting third-party effects, including geometric structure, rendered visual information, and system measurements. At a higher level, the study asks how much information relevant to a target variable is captured by different representations.

Who Pays for My Accuracy?

Manuscript

This study uses paired counterfactual interventions to examine how the execution strategy of one task can influence the model accuracy of another. The results show that aggregate completion metrics can hide directional, task-level changes in predictive accuracy.
